Every language has a grammatical framework — a set of structural rules that govern how words combine to form meaningful sentences. For Chinese learners of English, this framework presents genuine challenges: English uses tense inflection, articles, and relative clauses in ways that have no direct equivalent in Chinese. Systematic grammar study connects the scattered rules into a coherent whole, making both reading comprehension and writing more reliable.

The core insight from the source notes: **every English sentence, no matter how complex, reduces to simple sentences**. A simple sentence is "something + does something" — subject plus predicate. Everything else (compound sentences, complex sentences, clauses) is built on this foundation.

## Sentence Structure

English sentences are classified by structure into three types:

1. **Simple sentences** — a single independent clause: one subject + one predicate verb
2. **Compound sentences** — two or more independent clauses joined by coordinating conjunctions
3. **Complex sentences** — a main clause plus one or more subordinate (dependent) clauses

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Simple Sentences: The 5 Basic Patterns">
    All simple sentences follow one of five patterns, determined by the type of predicate verb:

    **Pattern 1 — S + V (intransitive)**

    The verb requires no object.

    > He **sleeps**. / The sun **rises**. / She **laughed**.

    **Pattern 2 — S + V + O (monotransitive)**

    The verb takes one direct object.

    > She **bought** a dress. / I **read** the book. / They **built** a house.

    **Pattern 3 — S + V + IO + DO (ditransitive)**

    The verb takes both an indirect and a direct object.

    > I **teach** you English. / She **sent** him a letter. / He **gave** me a gift.

    **Pattern 4 — S + V + O + OC (complex transitive)**

    The verb takes an object plus an object complement (which describes the object).

    > Peter **considers** you smart. / They **elected** her president. / We **found** the problem difficult.

    **Pattern 5 — S + V + P (linking verb + subject complement)**

    The linking verb connects the subject to a describing complement (the predicate adjective or noun).

    > He **is** tall. / The soup **smells** nice. / She **became** a doctor.

    <Note>
    The familiar "subject–verb–object" pattern is Pattern 2. "Subject–linking verb–complement" (主系表) is Pattern 5. These two patterns account for the vast majority of English sentences.
    </Note>
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Compound Sentences">
    Two independent clauses of equal weight joined by a **coordinating conjunction** (FANBOYS: *for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so*):

    > He likes coffee, **and** she likes tea.

    > She wanted to go to the park, **but** it was raining.

    > He studied for the test, **yet** he didn't pass.

    > It was getting late, **so** we left.

    The semicolon can replace a coordinating conjunction when the relationship is clear: *He was tired; he went to bed.*
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Complex Sentences and Clauses">
    A complex sentence contains a **main clause** and one or more **subordinate clauses**. The subordinate clause acts as a sentence component — subject, object, attribute, or adverbial — and is thus named accordingly.

    | Clause type | Sentence function | Example |
    |-------------|-------------------|---------|
    | Subject clause | subject of main verb | **That she left** surprised me. |
    | Object clause | object of main verb | I know **that he is honest**. |
    | Predicative clause | subject complement | The problem is **that we lack time**. |
    | Attributive clause | modifier of a noun | The book **that I read** is great. |
    | Adverbial clause | modifier of the verb | I'll go **if it doesn't rain**. |
    | Appositive clause | restates a noun | The news **that he won** excited us. |

    Noun clauses (subject, object, predicative, appositive) can often be introduced by *that*, *whether*, or *wh-* words. Attributive (relative) clauses use *who, whom, which, that, whose, when, where, why*.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

## Verb Tenses

English marks time and aspect through verb forms. Chinese has no grammatical tense — it uses time words instead. This makes tenses one of the most common error sources for Chinese learners.

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Simple Tenses">
    **Simple Present** — habitual actions, general truths, scheduled future events

    > She **studies** every evening. / Water **boils** at 100°C. / The train **leaves** at 8.

    Form: base form (third-person singular adds *-s*).

    **Simple Past** — completed actions at a specific past time

    > He **visited** London last year. / She **didn't come** to class.

    Form: *-ed* for regular verbs; irregular forms must be memorized (*go→went*, *see→saw*, *take→took*).

    **Simple Future** — actions that will happen

    > I **will call** you tomorrow. / She **is going to** study abroad.

    Note: *will* expresses a decision made at the moment of speaking; *be going to* expresses a prior plan.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Perfect Tenses">
    **Present Perfect** — a past action with present relevance; an unspecified past experience; an action that started in the past and continues now

    > I **have read** that book. (unspecified past) / She **has lived** here for ten years. (ongoing)

    Trigger words: *already, yet, just, ever, never, since, for, recently*.

    **Past Perfect** — the earlier of two past events

    > When I arrived, she **had already left**.

    **Future Perfect** — completed before a future point

    > By next month, I **will have finished** the project.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Progressive (Continuous) Tenses">
    Progressive tenses emphasize an action **in progress** at a specific moment.

    **Present Progressive** — happening now; a temporary activity; a future arrangement

    > She **is studying** right now. / I **am meeting** him tomorrow.

    **Past Progressive** — in progress at a specific past moment, often interrupted by another event

    > I **was reading** when the phone rang.

    **Present Perfect Progressive** — started in the past, ongoing now, with visible results

    > He **has been running** — he looks exhausted.

    <Warning>
    **Stative verbs** do not normally take progressive form: *know, believe, understand, want, love, hate, see, hear, belong, contain*. Use simple tenses with these verbs.
    > ✗ I **am knowing** the answer.
    > ✓ I **know** the answer.
    </Warning>
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

## Passive Voice

The passive voice shifts focus from the doer of an action to the receiver. It is formed with **be + past participle**.

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Passive Voice Formation">
    | Active | Passive |
    |--------|---------|
    | Simple present: *They build houses.* | *Houses **are built**.* |
    | Simple past: *They built the bridge.* | *The bridge **was built**.* |
    | Present perfect: *They have completed the work.* | *The work **has been completed**.* |
    | Future: *They will announce results.* | *Results **will be announced**.* |
    | Modal: *Someone must fix the machine.* | *The machine **must be fixed**.* |

    The agent (doer) can be expressed with *by*: *The report **was written** by the team.*
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="When to Use Passive Voice">
    Use passive voice when:

    - The agent is unknown: *My wallet **was stolen**.*
    - The agent is obvious or unimportant: *The results **were published** in 2024.*
    - The receiver is more important than the doer: *The patient **was examined** carefully.*
    - Writing in a formal or scientific register: *The samples **were heated** to 100°C.*

    <Tip>
    In Chinese, passive meaning is often conveyed by context alone or by 被 (*bèi*). English requires explicit passive verb forms, so passive constructions need to be deliberately practiced.
    </Tip>
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

## Conditionals

Conditional sentences express if–then relationships. English has four main conditional types based on the degree of reality or possibility.

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Zero Conditional — General Truths">
    **Structure:** If + simple present, simple present

    Used for facts that are always true when the condition is met.

    > **If** you heat water to 100°C, it **boils**.

    > **If** I don't sleep enough, I **feel** tired.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="First Conditional — Real Future Possibility">
    **Structure:** If + simple present, will + base verb

    Used for real or likely future situations.

    > **If** it rains tomorrow, we **will** cancel the picnic.

    > **If** you study hard, you **will** pass the exam.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Second Conditional — Unreal Present or Future">
    **Structure:** If + simple past, would + base verb

    Used for hypothetical or unlikely situations (not true now).

    > **If** I had more time, I **would** learn piano. (But I don't have time.)

    > **If** she were here, she **would** help us. (*Were* is used for all persons in formal English.)
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Third Conditional — Unreal Past">
    **Structure:** If + past perfect, would have + past participle

    Used for hypothetical situations in the past — things that did not happen.

    > **If** I had studied harder, I **would have passed** the exam. (But I didn't study hard enough.)

    > **If** she **had taken** the job, she **would have moved** to Beijing.

    <Note>
    Mixed conditionals combine second and third conditionals: *If I **had studied** medicine (third — past), I **would be** a doctor now (second — present result).*
    </Note>
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

## Relative Clauses

Relative clauses (attributive clauses) modify nouns using relative pronouns: *who, whom, which, that, whose, when, where, why*.

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Defining vs. Non-Defining Relative Clauses">
    **Defining (restrictive):** identifies which person or thing is meant. No commas.

    > The student **who scored highest** won the scholarship. (Which student? The one who scored highest.)

    **Non-defining (non-restrictive):** adds extra information about a person or thing already identified. Set off with commas.

    > My sister, **who lives in Shanghai**, is a doctor. (We already know which sister.)

    <Warning>
    In non-defining clauses, **never use *that*** — use *who* or *which*:
    > ✗ My sister, **that** lives in Shanghai, is a doctor.
    > ✓ My sister, **who** lives in Shanghai, is a doctor.
    </Warning>
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Relative Pronoun Selection">
    | Antecedent | Subject | Object | Possessive |
    |-----------|---------|--------|-----------|
    | Person | *who* / *that* | *who(m)* / *that* | *whose* |
    | Thing | *which* / *that* | *which* / *that* | *whose* / *of which* |
    | Place | *where* | — | — |
    | Time | *when* | — | — |
    | Reason | *why* | — | — |

    The relative pronoun can be **omitted** when it is the object of the clause:

    > The book **(that) I read** was fascinating. (object → *that* omissible)

    > The person **who helped me** was kind. (subject → *who* cannot be omitted)
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

## Parts of Speech

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Nouns">
    Nouns name people, things, places, or concepts. Key issues for Chinese learners:

    **Countable vs. uncountable:** Uncountable nouns (*water, information, advice, furniture, progress*) do not take *a/an* and have no plural form.

    > ✗ an information / ✓ a piece of information

    **Articles with nouns:** Use *a/an* for first mention of a countable noun; *the* for specific or previously mentioned nouns; no article for most uncountable and plural generic nouns.

    > I saw **a** dog. **The** dog was barking.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Pronouns">
    Personal pronouns: *I, you, he, she, it, we, they* (subject); *me, you, him, her, it, us, them* (object).

    Reflexive pronouns (*myself, yourself, himself...*) are used when the subject and object are the same: *She hurt **herself**.*

    Relative pronouns introduce relative clauses (see above).

    Indefinite pronouns: *someone, everyone, nobody, anything*. These take singular verbs.

    > **Everyone** in the class **is** ready. (not *are*)
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Articles">
    The three articles: **a** (indefinite, before consonant sounds), **an** (indefinite, before vowel sounds), **the** (definite).

    Common rules:
    - First mention → *a/an*; subsequent mentions → *the*
    - Unique things → *the* (*the sun, the moon, the Internet*)
    - Superlatives → *the* (*the best, the most important*)
    - Musical instruments → *the* (*play the piano*)
    - No article: most proper nouns, languages (*English*), meals (*breakfast*), sports (*play basketball*)

    <Note>
    Chinese has no article system. Article errors are among the most frequent mistakes Chinese learners make in writing. Deliberate practice with controlled exercises is needed.
    </Note>
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Adjectives and Adverbs">
    **Adjectives** modify nouns and pronouns. Position: before the noun (*a **tall** building*) or after a linking verb (*The building is **tall***).

    Order of adjectives before a noun (OSASCOMP): Opinion → Size → Age → Shape → Color → Origin → Material → Purpose
    > a **beautiful small old round green French silver** whittling knife

    **Adverbs** modify verbs, adjectives, other adverbs, or whole sentences. Many are formed by adding *-ly* to adjectives: *quick → quickly*, *careful → carefully*. Irregular: *good → well*, *fast → fast*, *hard → hard*.

    Comparative and superlative: short adjectives add *-er/-est*; longer adjectives use *more/most*.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Prepositions">
    Prepositions express relationships of time, place, direction, and manner. Common errors:

    | Incorrect | Correct | Note |
    |-----------|---------|------|
    | *in Monday* | *on Monday* | Days of the week use *on* |
    | *at the morning* | *in the morning* | Parts of the day use *in* |
    | *depend of* | *depend on* | Verb + preposition collocations |
    | *interested about* | *interested in* | *interested in*, not *about* |
    | *married with* | *married to* | *get married to* someone |

    Key collocations: *good **at***, *afraid **of***, *responsible **for***, *similar **to***, *different **from***, *capable **of***, *consist **of***, *result **in*** vs. *result **from***.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

## Common Mistakes for Chinese Learners

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Subject-Verb Agreement">
    The verb must agree with the subject in number and person.

    > ✗ Everyone **are** here.
    > ✓ Everyone **is** here.

    > ✗ The team **are** winning.
    > ✓ The team **is** winning. (treating the team as a unit)

    Compound subjects joined by *and* take a plural verb. But *either...or / neither...nor* take a verb that agrees with the **nearest subject**:
    > Neither the students nor the teacher **was** present.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Tense Consistency">
    Chinese relies on time words (*yesterday, now, tomorrow*) rather than verb endings to convey time. English requires both.

    > ✗ Yesterday I **go** to the library.
    > ✓ Yesterday I **went** to the library.

    In narrative writing, maintain a consistent tense. Shifting between past and present tense without reason is a common error.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Missing or Extra Articles">
    > ✗ She is **a** honest person.
    > ✓ She is **an** honest person. (*honest* begins with a vowel sound)

    > ✗ I like **the** music. (music in general)
    > ✓ I like **music**. (generic → no article)

    > ✗ The life is short.
    > ✓ Life is short. (abstract concept → no article)
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Plural and Countability">
    > ✗ I have many **homeworks**.
    > ✓ I have a lot of **homework**. (uncountable)

    > ✗ She gave me an **advice**.
    > ✓ She gave me a piece of **advice** / some **advice**.

    Commonly uncountable: *advice, information, knowledge, news, furniture, equipment, luggage, research, progress, traffic, weather*.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Non-Finite Verb Confusion">
    English has three non-finite verb forms: **infinitive** (*to do*), **gerund** (*doing*), and **past participle** (*done*).

    Some verbs are followed by infinitives, some by gerunds, some by both (sometimes with different meanings):

    | Verb + infinitive | Verb + gerund |
    |------------------|--------------|
    | want **to do** | enjoy **doing** |
    | decide **to do** | avoid **doing** |
    | hope **to do** | finish **doing** |
    | refuse **to do** | suggest **doing** |
    | manage **to do** | consider **doing** |

    Meaning-changing verbs:
    - *I **remember to lock** the door.* (future action, unforgotten)
    - *I **remember locking** the door.* (past action, recalled)
    - *She **stopped to rest**.* (stopped in order to rest)
    - *She **stopped resting**.* (ceased resting)
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

## Word Order

English is a subject-verb-object language with relatively fixed word order. The most common mistakes involve placing adverbs, indirect objects, and modifiers incorrectly.

**Adverb placement:**
- Frequency adverbs (*always, usually, often, sometimes, rarely, never*) come **before the main verb** but **after *be* or auxiliary verbs**.
  > She **always** arrives early. / He **is usually** late.

**Question word order:**
- In questions, the auxiliary verb comes before the subject (subject-auxiliary inversion):
  > Do you like coffee? / Have you finished?
- Indirect questions (embedded questions) revert to statement word order:
  > I wonder **where she is**. (not *where is she*)
